### Two facts on every repair order that never meet

**The customer is billed book hours at your door rate.** Every accounting package can check that,
and it is what people mean when they say a shop bills correctly.

**The tech takes however long they take.** None of those packages record that against the book.

So a shop can bill every ticket exactly right, all week, and still not have paid for the bay. The
door rate was correct. The book time was correct. And the job that books at 1.8 took four hours,
which is the number nobody wrote down next to it.

This sets the two side by side, per ticket and for the week.

### The efficiency figure is the one that decides the week

What book said the job takes, against what the clock said it took.

That ratio is your shop's efficiency, and it is the number that turns a correctly billed week into a
week that paid or a week that did not. It is not in your accounting, it is not on the repair order,
and it is not in the flat rate guide.

### It works the rate over the tickets, not across the percentages

This is technical and it changes the answer, so it is worth a minute.

Averaging the per-ticket efficiency percentages weights a twenty-minute job the same as a full day.
Fifteen quick tickets that ran ahead of book and three big ones that ran badly behind produces a
comforting average and a week that lost money.

This sums the book hours and sums the clock hours and divides once, over the same tickets. That is
what the shop actually ran at. The gap between that figure and the simple average tells you
something on its own — a wide one means your good numbers are on your small jobs.

### A comeback is what it is

No bill. No book time. Clock hours the shop ate.

Treating it as a ticket that came in short is the tempting shortcut and it hides the thing you most
want to see. A comeback is not a job that went slowly. It is time spent with no revenue against it at
all, and it belongs in the arithmetic as exactly that.

Counted properly, a run of comebacks shows up as what it is: hours disappearing with nothing on the
other side of them.

### What it replaces

The sense that the week was busy and the month was not good, with no way to connect the two. Or the
efficiency number from a management system, computed by averaging percentages, which flatters a shop
that does a lot of small work.

You set your door rate once. Each week is the tickets, with book hours and clock hours on each.

### Who it is for

Repair shops, body shops, marine and equipment service — anywhere work is quoted from a flat rate
guide and performed by a person whose actual hours are the shop's real cost.

### What it will not do

**It sets no target efficiency**, and knows nothing about what any other shop runs. What a good
number looks like is different in every bay — it depends on your work mix, your equipment, your
techs' experience and what kind of jobs come through your door. Anybody quoting you a figure for the
trade is selling something.

**It does not evaluate a technician.** It reports what tickets did. Whether a slow job was the tech,
the vehicle, a missing part or a wrong diagnosis is not something the arithmetic can see, and a
number presented as a verdict on a person is how a shop loses good people.

**It does not price a job.** That is a different sheet.

**Nothing you type is sent anywhere.** The arithmetic runs in your browser. Ticket data and hours stay
on the machine you typed them on. Close the tab and it is gone.
