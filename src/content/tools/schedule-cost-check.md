### A schedule is a spending decision, and it gets priced afterwards

You write the week on a Wednesday. You find out what it cost you eleven days later, when payroll runs
and the number is already spent.

Nothing about that is necessary. The schedule is on the board, everyone's rate is known, and
multiplying the two is arithmetic a phone can do. It just never gets done at the moment it could
still change anything, because doing it by hand means twenty-two people at eight rates across seven
days, and there is always something more urgent on a Wednesday.

This prices the week while the week is still a draft.

### Broken out by role, because one number hides the thing that moved

A single payroll figure for the week tells you almost nothing. It went up — where?

A kitchen that crept up two shifts is invisible inside a total that also contains a slow front of
house. Split by role and the drift has an address. That is the difference between a number you look
at and a number you act on.

### Two figures are yours, and neither can come from anywhere else

**What you expect to take.** Not what you took last week — what you expect, which is a judgment
containing a holiday, a road closure, a festival, and the fact that you know your street.

**What share of that you have decided payroll should be.** This is the one people expect software to
supply, and it is the one software has no business supplying. What your labor ought to run at
depends on whether you are counter service or full service, whether the owner works the floor,
what your rent is, and what kind of business you are trying to build. A number pulled from a survey
of other people's restaurants is not an answer to that question. It is a different question's answer,
wearing a percentage sign.

From those two, this works out what the week affords and how far above or below the schedule sits —
in money as well as in points, because "one point over" means nothing until you know it is four
hundred dollars.

### Three things it deliberately does not compute

**It will not guess next week's sales**, because a tool that projected takings from last week's
would be selling you a guess dressed as arithmetic, and you would schedule against it.

**It carries no target of its own.** There is no default here, no suggested range, and no gentle
nudge toward a figure. If you have not decided what your payroll share should be, this sheet will not
decide it for you, because that decision is about your business.

**It computes nobody's take-home.** Tips, taxes, overtime, tip credit and withholding are specific to
you and to where you are, and this will not print a number it cannot show you the working for. This
is what the schedule costs the business, not what anybody takes home.

### What it replaces

The version of this that happens after the fact, in the accounting, when the week is over and the
decision cannot be revisited. Or the spreadsheet that would do it, if somebody rebuilt it every time
a rate changed.

You enter your people and their rates once. Every week after that is the hours as written.

### Who it is for

Restaurants, bars, retail and salons — anywhere a schedule is written by hand each week and the wage
bill is the largest thing you control.

### What it will not do

**It will not tell you the schedule is wrong.** It tells you what it costs against what you said you
expect. Whether that is too much is a decision that includes things a page cannot see — a new cook
who needs the hours, a Saturday you cannot afford to be short on.

**It will not compare you to other businesses.** Nothing here comes from outside your own numbers.

**Nothing you type is sent anywhere.** The arithmetic runs in your browser. Wage rates are among the
most sensitive things a small business holds, and none of it leaves the machine you typed it on.
Close the tab and it is gone.
