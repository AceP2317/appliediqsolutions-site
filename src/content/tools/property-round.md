### The properties that lose money are the far ones, not the slow ones

This is the finding, and it is almost always the same finding.

A route business charges per property and pays per hour, and those two numbers never appear on the
same piece of paper. So when a round is not making what it should, the search goes to the slow jobs —
the big lawn, the awkward one with the gate, the house with the dogs.

Count the drive and the picture changes. A twenty-minute drive to a thirty-minute job is a
fifty-minute job, whatever the invoice says. The property that pays well for the work and sits eleven
minutes past the last stop is the one quietly costing you, and no invoice will ever show it, because
the drive is not on the invoice.

### Per crew hour, with the drive in it

Each property's price divided by the crew time it actually takes, drive included. One number per
stop, in money per crew hour, so every property on the round is comparable to every other one on the
one measure that matters.

That is it. There is no scoring, no ranking of customers, no traffic light. A number per stop and the
two figures it came from.

### The round's own rate is worked over the rows, not averaged across them

This one is technical and it changes the answer.

Averaging the per-property rates weights a ten-minute stop the same as a two-hour one. Twenty small
jobs at a good rate and four big ones at a poor rate produces a comforting average and a bad week.

This sums the money and sums the time and divides once, over the same rows. That is the rate the
round actually ran at. It is often worse than the average, and the gap between the two numbers is
itself worth looking at — a wide gap means your good rates are on your small stops.

### It also says what is past its interval

The same date arithmetic that tells a clinic which patients are overdue tells you which properties
are past when you said you would be back.

A route drifts. A week of rain, a truck down, a crew short, and two streets slip without anybody
deciding they should. This lists them by how far past they are, furthest first, which is the order
you would actually work them.

### What it replaces

The suspicion that something on the round is not paying, which everybody who runs a route has, and
which nobody has the evening to prove. Or the spreadsheet with drive time left out of it, because
drive time is the annoying thing to collect and the whole reason the spreadsheet says everything is
fine.

You set your properties, your prices and your times once. Each pass after that is a read.

### Who it is for

Lawn crews, cleaners, pest control, pool service, mobile repair — anything worked as a route, where
the money is priced per stop and the cost is paid per hour.

### What it will not do

**It will never tell you to drop a customer.** This is the important one. A property that loses money
can be the reason you got the street, the one the neighbor saw, or the elderly customer you keep
because you keep them. A page cannot see any of that, and a tool that ranked customers by profit and
put a red mark next to one would be handing you a decision it has no standing to make.

**It suggests no price and no interval.** What a stop is worth and how often you go back are yours.

**It names no rate you should be hitting.** There is no target in here, from a survey or anywhere
else.

**Nothing you type is sent anywhere.** The arithmetic runs in your browser. Your customer list and
your prices stay on the machine you typed them on. Close the tab and it is gone.
