### Two numbers that must never be added together

This is the arithmetic mistake that makes every no-show figure you have ever seen too big.

**The missed billing** is what the appointment would have brought in. Real money that did not
arrive.

**The empty room** is the overhead that hour cost you anyway — rent, light, a therapist on salary
standing there.

Add them and you have counted one hour of overhead twice. The room was already paid for. It was paid
for at nine in the morning whether anybody walked in or not, and it is not an additional loss caused
by the person who did not come.

The summed number is bigger, it is more alarming, and it is the number that gets quoted in articles
about how much no-shows cost the profession. It is also wrong. These two are shown side by side and
never summed.

### A refilled slot recovers what the replacement billed, not what the original would have

The slot got filled. Good. It rarely gets filled with the same money.

A last-minute patient is often a shorter visit, a different code, or somebody who was already coming
and just moved earlier. So the loss is the difference between what would have billed and what did —
not zero, and not the whole thing.

Treating a refilled slot as fully recovered is the other direction of the same error, and it makes
your figure too small in exactly the weeks the front desk worked hardest.

### Three things belong to your practice and to nobody else

**How much notice counts as late.** Twenty-four hours, forty-eight, the day before by five.

**Whether a late cancel is charged like a no-show.** Some practices do, some do not, and some do for
some payers.

**How much the fee is.**

A no-show gives no notice at all, so it falls inside any window you set. A cancel is late only under
the hours you chose. That distinction is applied here rather than assumed, because assuming it would
put your policy quietly into every figure without you having agreed to it.

### There is no name field

A booking reference is as far as this goes. No patient name, nothing clinical, no reason for the
visit.

That keeps it a scheduling arithmetic sheet rather than a record, and it keeps it away from a
question nobody wants: whether a piece of software is quietly building a profile of who does not turn
up.

### What it replaces

The suspicion that the gaps in the week are costing real money, which every practice has, and the
absence of any number to put against it. Or a figure from an article about the profession, which is
about somebody else's practice.

You set your three policy numbers once. Each week is the day sheet.

### Who it is for

Physical therapy, dental, medical, behavioral health — any practice selling booked hours where a
gap cannot be resold at short notice.

### What it will not do

**It suggests no fee.** What you charge for a late cancel depends on your patients, your community
and your own read on whether charging helps.

**It scores nobody.** There is no repeat-offender list, no reliability rating, and no field to build
one from.

**It compares your week to no published rate.** Whatever the profession's average no-show figure is,
it is not about your practice and it will not appear here.

**Nothing you type is sent anywhere.** The arithmetic runs in your browser. Even holding only booking
references, this is scheduling data about a healthcare practice, and none of it leaves the machine
you typed it on. Close the tab and it is gone.
