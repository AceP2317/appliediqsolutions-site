### A statement that nets to zero can still be wrong

This is the whole reason to check line by line rather than checking a total.

A carrier statement arrives. You compare the bottom of it against what you expected, the two are
close enough, and the statement goes in the drawer. Inside it, one policy paid at a rate from a
contract that ended in March and another paid at a rate that was never yours. One is short, one is
over, and they very nearly cancel.

The total told you nothing. It could not have told you anything — that is what a total is for.

This sheet applies your own schedule to every line, shows the difference on each, and lists every
short line separately. A statement that comes out even can still hand you a page of lines that do
not agree.

### The schedule is yours, and it is more specific than a rate

**A rate belongs to a carrier and a product together.** Not to a carrier. The same company pays
differently on a new personal auto policy than on a renewal, and differently again on a commercial
line. A schedule with one number per carrier is a schedule that will be wrong most of the time.

**New and renewal are different numbers.** Sometimes very different. A schedule that does not carry
both is one somebody is going to have to remember, which means somebody is going to forget.

**What the rate runs against.** Written premium, earned premium, premium net of fees and taxes.
A percentage is meaningless until that is settled, and it is settled in the contract rather than by
convention.

Those live in agreements you signed, in a folder, and there is no service anywhere that can read
them for you. That is why this is a sheet you fill in once rather than something somebody sells by
the month.

### The line that does not match anything

A policy arrives on a statement carrying a carrier and a product that are not on your schedule.

This does not guess. It does not fall back to the nearest rate, does not use the carrier's other
product, and does not quietly price it at zero and let it disappear into a total. It reports the
line, says it could not be priced, and leaves it unpriced.

That refusal is the point. A tool that guessed would produce a confident number, and a confident
wrong number on a commission statement is worse than no number, because somebody will act on it.

### What it replaces

The spreadsheet with the rates typed into it, which was right when it was built and has since had a
contract change nobody propagated. Or the check that does not happen at all, because the statement
is forty pages and there is no version of doing this by hand that fits into a week.

You enter your schedule once — carrier, product, basis, rate. The sheet keeps it. Every statement
after that is a paste and a read.

### Who it is for

Insurance agencies and brokers — anywhere commission arrives on somebody else's statement, computed
by somebody else's system, against contracts only you hold.

### What it will not do

**It will not suggest a rate.** It does not know what your carriers pay, what other agencies are
paid, or what a rate ought to be. It applies the schedule you entered, from the contracts you signed.

**It takes no view on what to do about a difference.** A short line is a phone call, and whether that
call is worth making is a judgment about a relationship. This sheet finds the line. It does not
tell you what the line means.

**It will not net a shortfall away.** Overpayments and underpayments are shown separately and never
allowed to cancel, because the canceling is the failure that made this necessary.

**Nothing you paste is sent anywhere.** The arithmetic runs in your browser. A commission statement
carries policy numbers and client names, and none of it leaves the machine you are sitting at. Close
the tab and it is gone.
