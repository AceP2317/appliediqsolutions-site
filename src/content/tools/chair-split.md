### Product comes off first, and the order is not cosmetic

Take the product charge off services before the split, and the chair pays for the color once.

Take it after, and the chair pays for the color and then hands the shop a percentage of money that
was already spent on the color. Same two numbers, same two people, and a settlement that differs
every single week.

Most shops have decided this. Few have written it down anywhere, which means it lives in whoever
does the settlements, and it changes quietly when that person is on holiday.

### Four things this shop settled, and no software can supply

**The product charge.** A flat amount, a percentage of the service, or per-service by type. Some
shops do not charge it at all and build it into the split instead.

**The service split.** Whatever rate this shop landed on. It is often not the same for every chair,
and it is often not the same as it was two years ago.

**Retail, which almost never splits at the service rate.** A shop that runs both at one number is
making a decision, and usually not on purpose.

**Booth rent, which sits outside all of the above.** A chair on rent pays for the chair. They are not
in the split at all, and mixing the two models on one sheet is where hand-done settlements go wrong.

Those four are why a subscription cannot do this. A platform serving every salon in the country has
to pick a default for each, and the moment it picks, it is settling somebody's week on rules they
never agreed to.

### The card fee, split on what each chair actually ran

Splitting the card fee evenly is the easy thing and it is wrong.

A chair that ran eleven hundred dollars through the reader and a chair that ran two hundred did not
cost the shop the same in processing. Charge them the same and the quiet chair is paying for money
it never took — a small amount, every week, forever, to the person least able to notice.

This shares the fee on what each chair actually ran. The leftover pennies go out by largest
remainder, the same routine the tip out calculator uses, so the shares add back to the fee the shop
was actually charged rather than to something close to it.

### When a chair owes the shop money

It happens. A light week against a product charge, or a chair on rent who took almost nothing.

This says so on the line, in words. It does not print a minus sign and move on, and it does not
quietly floor the number at zero and let the sheet look finished — because a settlement that hides
a shortfall is one somebody discovers a month later, when it is three shortfalls and a conversation
nobody wants to have.

**It takes no cut of tips. Ever.** Tips are not in the split, are not in the product charge, and are
not touched by the card fee arithmetic. That is not a setting.

### What it replaces

The settlement done on a pad on a Sunday, or the spreadsheet built by somebody who has left. Both
work until a chair changes model, a rate changes, or somebody inserts a row.

You enter your rules once. Every week after that is the week's numbers and who worked.

### Who it is for

Salons, barber shops, nail and lash studios — anywhere a chair is rented or a week is split, and the
terms were set in this shop rather than handed down by a franchise.

### What it will not do

**It will not suggest a rent, a split or a product charge.** There is nothing here to recommend.
What a chair is worth in this shop is the thing you decided, and deciding it is the part of running
a salon that is actually yours.

**It does not know employment law where you are and does not check it.** Whether somebody is a
renter or an employee has consequences this sheet knows nothing about. It divides money the way you
told it to. Arithmetic, not advice.

**It will not print a number it cannot stand behind.** Where a figure cannot be read, it names the
row and the shape it could not handle rather than working around it.

**Nothing you type is sent anywhere.** The arithmetic runs in your browser — no account, no upload,
nothing stored on anybody's server. Close the tab and it is gone.
