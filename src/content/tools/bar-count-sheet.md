### One pour cost hides whichever thing moved

A bar that reports a single pour cost is averaging three businesses together.

Liquor, beer and wine do not behave alike. They are bought differently, they spoil differently, they
get given away differently, and they move at different speeds. Blend them into one percentage and a
liquor problem is cushioned by a good beer month, or a beer problem disappears behind wine that
barely moved.

This counts each kind against the sales you actually rang for that kind. Three numbers, not one. The
week a number moves, you know which shelf to go and look at.

### What poured is a subtraction, not an estimate

Opening count, plus what came in, minus closing count. That is what left the building.

It is a subtraction and this keeps it a subtraction. There is no model in here, nothing is smoothed,
and no line is adjusted toward what it usually does. If the number is strange, the number is strange
and you go and look — which is the only thing that has ever found a real problem behind a bar.

Counts are held in half-units and money in whole cents, so a shelf of a hundred lines does not drift
a few cents at a time into a figure nobody trusts.

### The one thing it refuses to calculate, and why

**It will not compare what you used against what you theoretically should have used.**

That comparison is the headline feature of every bar inventory product sold, and it needs two things
you do not have: a recipe for every drink you make, and a real pour size for every one of them —
not the one on the spec card, the one your bartenders actually pour on a Friday at eleven.

Without those, the variance number is arithmetic on a guess. It looks precise, it is quotable, and it
sends people to have conversations with staff about a figure that was invented. This sheet does not
print it, because a confident wrong number is worse than a blank space.

What it will tell you is what left, what it cost, and what that came to against what you rang. Those
are all subtractions on things you counted.

### Par is yours, and the order rounds up

The reorder is against the pars you set, and it rounds up, because nobody has ever ordered two
thirds of a bottle.

What a par should be is not a question with an answer that lives outside your bar. It depends on your
delivery days, your storage, your cash position and what you are unwilling to run out of on a
Saturday. This sheet applies the pars you set. It does not suggest them, and it does not drift them
based on last month.

### What it replaces

The clipboard count that gets typed into a spreadsheet the next day, sometimes. Or the count that
happens every third week because the full one takes four hours and there is never a four-hour gap.

You set your shelf, your costs and your pars once. Every count after that is walking the bar with a
tablet and reading the answer at the end of it.

### Who it is for

Bars, restaurants, breweries and taprooms — anywhere the shelf is worth enough that what leaves it
untracked matters, and where the pars and the costs were set by the person who buys.

### What it will not do

**It will not tell you what a pour cost should be.** There is no number here to hand you. What yours
ought to be depends on what you pour, what you charge and what kind of room you are running.

**It will not set a par.** See above. That is a decision about your own risk on a Saturday night.

**It will not compare you to anybody.** No survey figures, no category averages, nothing from
anywhere but your own counts.

**Nothing you type is sent anywhere.** The arithmetic runs in your browser. Your costs and your
volumes are commercially yours, and they stay on the machine you counted on. Close the tab and it is
gone.
