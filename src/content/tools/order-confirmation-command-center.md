### One confirmation-rate number hides everywhere the misses actually live

Confirmation rate is a percentage on a slide. It went down. That sentence contains no information
anybody can act on.

Underneath it are thousands of units that could not be confirmed on the date a customer asked for,
and they failed for completely different reasons. A container still at sea. A quality hold. Demand
nobody saw coming. Stock physically present but sitting in a staging buffer with the wrong status.

Those four need four different people and four different actions. Rolled into one percentage they
need nobody, which is why the number goes down again next month.

### Every unconfirmed unit gets exactly one cause

The tool walks each unit through an ordered set of tests and stops at the first one that fits.
First match wins, and the order of the tests is the design.

That single-owner rule matters more than it sounds. A unit that could plausibly be blamed on three
things and is blamed on all three gets counted three times, and then the totals add up to more
problems than there are orders. Worse, nobody owns it, because everybody can point at somebody else.

One unit, one cause, one owner. The percentages then sum to the real shortfall and each bucket is a
number somebody can be asked about.

### Five buckets, and then all the way down to the line

Causes roll into five groups: forecasting, logistics, supply, customer, and everything else.

From there you drill. Category, then the specific cause inside it, then the individual order lines
carrying it. The top level is for the meeting; the bottom level is the work.

### It scores customers, not just causes

A shortfall spread thinly across two hundred customers is a bad month. The same shortfall
concentrated on four customers is a relationship problem, and it is often invisible in the totals.

The tool ranks customers by how badly their own service is failing rather than by how many units
they represent. A small account being missed every single week is a louder signal than a large
account missed once.

### It ends in a worklist, ranked, with an action beside each line

Each cause carries a recommended next step, and the export is ordered so the top of the list is
where the effort goes first.

That is the difference between analysis and a tool. A report tells you the shape of the problem. A
worklist tells one person what to do before lunch.

### What this page is

A working demo, not something for sale.

It runs on **Northpoint Manufacturing**, an invented company with invented customers, invented order
lines and invented causes. No real order book is behind any of it.

It is here as evidence of the caliber behind the smaller tools that *are* sold — one local business,
one narrow job. This is the deep end of the same practice.

### What it will not do

**It sets no confirmation-rate target.** What good looks like depends on your lead times and what
you promised. Nothing here will suggest a figure.

**It does not decide the cause order for you.** The waterfall's sequence encodes a real judgment
about which cause owns a unit when several could. In a real deployment that order is agreed with
the people who will be held to it, before a single number is published.

**It compares you to nobody.** No outside data, no peer set, nothing beyond the order lines in front
of it.

**A recommended action is a starting point, never an instruction.** The tool has no idea what
happened on the phone this morning.
