### Quoting forward is not checking a bill backwards

They look like the same arithmetic and they are not.

Checking a bill is subtraction — here is what was charged, here is what it should have been. You have
every number already and you are looking for a difference.

Quoting is a build. Hours you have not worked yet, at a rate, plus parts you have not bought yet,
plus a markup, plus whatever it costs to have the truck at the curb at all. Every one of those is a
decision, and they get made standing in somebody's kitchen with a phone in one hand.

That is where quotes go wrong. Not from bad arithmetic — from four decisions made quickly, in the
wrong order, with nothing to check them against.

### The four things, in the order they apply

**Labor.** Hours against your rate. The simple one.

**Parts.** What the supply house charged, plus the markup your shop settled on once and has kept
since. Shown as two figures rather than one, because a customer who asks what the part cost is asking
a reasonable question and you should be able to answer it without doing division in your head.

**The call-out minimum.** A fifteen-minute visit still pays for the truck being there and for the
hour of the day it consumed. Where a line was lifted to the minimum, this says so on the line — not
in a footnote at the bottom, on the line — because that is the number a customer queries.

**Travel.** Attached to the job it belongs to, not floating at the bottom of the quote where it looks
like padding.

### When the markup would price a part below cost

It happens more than you would think. A percentage markup, a part that came in dearer than the last
time, a number typed in a hurry.

This says so rather than quoting it. A quote that prices a part under what you paid for it is not a
tight quote, it is a mistake you are going to honor, and you will find it when the supply house
invoice arrives three weeks later.

### The two decisions nobody can make for you

**Your rate.** What an hour of your time is worth depends on your license, your years, your
insurance, your van, and what your town will bear.

**Your markup.** Same. It covers your carrying cost, your returns, your trips to the counter, and the
fact that you warranty the part.

Those two decisions are what a shop is actually paid to make. Any tool that suggested them would be
handing you somebody else's answer to your own business's question — and it would not know whether
that somebody else was making money.

This knows nothing about what anybody in your county charges, and it will not be adding it.

### What it replaces

The quote worked out on the back of a card in the van, and the one that comes in over the phone an
hour later after a lot of arithmetic. Or the spreadsheet on a laptop that does not come to the job.

You set your rate, your markup and your minimum once. Each quote is the hours and the parts.

### Who it is for

HVAC, plumbing, electrical, appliance repair, landscaping — any trade that prices a job before doing
it and lives or dies on whether that price was right.

### What it will not do

**It suggests no rate and no markup.** Stated twice, because it is the one thing everybody expects a
quoting tool to do.

**It does not know what the job involves.** It prices what you tell it. It has never seen the
crawlspace.

**It is not an invoice and not a contract.** It is the arithmetic of a price, with the working shown
so you can defend every line of it.

**Nothing you type is sent anywhere.** The arithmetic runs in your browser — which also means it
works in a basement with no signal. Close the tab and it is gone.
