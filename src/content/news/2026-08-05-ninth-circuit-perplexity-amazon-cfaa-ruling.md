title: "Appeals court says AI shopping agents don't 'access' the sites they visit — users do"

summary: >-
  A Ninth Circuit panel vacated a lower court injunction that had blocked
  Perplexity's Comet AI shopping assistant from interacting with Amazon's
  site, ruling that under the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act it is the user —
  not Perplexity — who "accesses" Amazon's computers when Comet acts on a
  signed-in user's behalf. The panel found Amazon was unlikely to succeed on
  its federal CFAA and California CDAFA claims and sent the case back to the
  district court, while explicitly calling the ruling narrow and not a new
  legal regime for agentic AI generally. Amazon said it disagrees with the
  decision and is weighing next steps.
implications: >-
  This is one of the first appellate rulings anywhere on what it means for
  an AI agent to "access" a website on a user's behalf, and it currently
  favors the agents: a business can't necessarily rely on unauthorized-access
  law alone to keep AI shopping or browsing tools off its site if a user
  directed the visit. Any business leaning on its terms of service or
  CFAA-style claims to block bot or agent traffic should treat this as
  unsettled, fast-moving law rather than a solved question.
angle: >-
  A website is one of the few customer channels a small business fully
  owns — but this ruling is a reminder that owning it doesn't mean
  controlling who or what visits on a customer's behalf. As agentic
  browsing spreads, that's a good reason to have an owner actually watching
  that landscape behind a site, not a template nobody revisits.
